# Ceremony Message Flows — A Reading Guide

This document walks through two ceremonies — the **bootstrap (keygen)**
ceremony that creates a fresh wallet, and a **routine signing**
ceremony — at the message level, in the form of sequence diagrams.
The goal is to make the temporal flow and multi-party choreography
legible without the cryptographic mechanics underneath.

The diagrams ignore transport — by the time you read a message in
the diagram, assume any AEAD wrapping, routing, fan-out, and queueing
has been done by the layers below. (How those layers work is in
`docs/recovery-exploration-harness-design.md` and
`signet/docs/05-noise-kk-session-design.md`.) What's drawn here is
the *conceptual* peer-to-peer exchange.

## Cast

| Symbol | Actor | What it holds | Available |
|--------|-------|---------------|-----------|
| **H** | Holder | Long-term identity key. After keygen: a share of the wallet. | When transacting. |
| **F** | Figure (issuer) | Long-term identity key. After keygen: a share of the wallet. | Always online. |
| **IC** | Independent Custodian | Long-term identity key. After keygen: a share of the wallet. | Offline by default; online for structural ceremonies. |

A **coordinator** role drives each ceremony — it issues the
`begin-*` instruction at the top of the diagram and collects
completion reports at the bottom. In the harness this is the
orchestrator, but the role rotates among parties in production
(the role can hold no secret material; it's purely a control-plane
job).

## Legend

```
 🔓 cleartext (anyone with access to the channel can read)
 🔒 encrypted between sender and recipient (no one else can read)
 🗝 secret material that NEVER appears on any wire (lives only on
    the party that holds it; encrypted at rest on disk)
 ──>     a one-way message
 ─⇋─    two-message handshake establishing a session
 ▷       summary of the collective state after a phase
```

## Bootstrap: trust and authenticated private channels

Before *any* multi-sig ceremony can run, the parties need two
things: a way to **identify each other** (so a peer can be sure it's
talking to the right party) and a way to **exchange messages
privately** (so a peer can speak to another peer without leaking the
content to anyone else routing the bytes).

These are **prerequisites** for the multi-sig protocol — not part
of it. The threshold-cryptography mathematics in the ceremonies
that follow assume "we have authenticated private channels between
each pair of peers" and otherwise pay the bootstrap no mind. The
specific mechanisms below (Ed25519 identities, Noise_KK sessions)
could be replaced wholesale by TLS + a PKI, by HSM-mediated
channels, by any number of alternatives — the multi-sig ceremonies
on top would not need to change.

The bootstrap has two pieces:

### Identity setup (one-time, at process startup)

Each party generates a **long-term Ed25519 keypair** for its own
use. The same keypair serves multiple purposes (one identity,
multiple uses):
- signing artifacts and ceremony outputs (Ed25519);
- pairwise transport encryption with peers (via the
  Ed25519↔X25519 birational map);
- deriving the local KEK that encrypts share files at rest.

Each party publishes its **public key** to the others. In this
harness, the bb wrapper announces it once at process startup; in
production a directory or out-of-band channel serves the same role.

```
   H  → others : identity-pubkey_H                                 🔓
   F  → others : identity-pubkey_F                                 🔓
   IC → others : identity-pubkey_IC                                🔓
```
▷ Each party holds its own long-term keypair (private kept local;
   public shared with peers). Identities are now known to everyone
   and **reused across every subsequent ceremony** — no per-ceremony
   identity setup needed.

### Per-ceremony privacy preamble

Every ceremony starts the same way: the coordinator issues a
`begin-*` instruction with the ceremony parameters, and the
participating peers establish fresh **pairwise transport keys**.

```
   coordinator → participants :  begin-<ceremony-type>             🔓
                                  (ceremony-id, peers, parameters,
                                   peer identity-pubkeys)


   per pair of participants:
       X ─⇋ Noise_KK ⇋── Y           →  TK_{X,Y}
```

▷ A forward-secret **transport key** `TK_{X,Y}` is established
   for each pair via a **Noise_KK X25519 key-agreement** handshake
   (the Noise_KK pattern from the Noise Protocol Framework,
   mechanized in this project as `signet.session`). Each pair
   derives its `TK_{X,Y}` from a chain of four X25519 Diffie–Hellman
   outputs combining **both parties' long-term identity keys**
   (the Ed25519 keypairs from identity setup, cross-converted to
   X25519) and **a fresh ephemeral X25519 keypair generated by each
   party for this ceremony**. The mixed long-term keys give mutual
   authentication; the mixed ephemerals give forward secrecy — when
   the ephemerals are deleted at ceremony end, no later compromise
   of the long-term keys can recover any `TK_{X,Y}`. All peer-to-peer
   messages after this point are AEAD-encrypted under the
   corresponding pair's `TK`.      🔒 from here on

Because this preamble is identical for every ceremony, the ceremony
descriptions below skip it and pick up at the first multi-sig-
specific phase. Assume that by the time you read **Phase 1** in any
ceremony, the begin instruction has been received and the pairwise
`TK_{X,Y}`s are in place.

## Ceremony 1 — Bootstrap (Keygen)

**Goal**: produce a fresh ECDSA wallet keypair such that no single
party holds the private key. End state: H, F, and IC each hold one
share of a 2-of-3 threshold signing scheme; all three know the
public key; the public key becomes the wallet's persistent identity.

The four phases below implement a **Pedersen-style Verifiable
Secret Sharing (VSS)** scheme: every party contributes a piece of
randomness via its own secret polynomial, and a publicly verifiable
commitment lets receivers detect tampering without learning the
secret. The same polynomial-vs-curve-point trick recurs in many
threshold-cryptography protocols (FROST, BLS threshold signatures,
KZG polynomial commitments) — once it's clear here, you'll
recognize the shape elsewhere.

After each phase, an arrow `▷` summarizes the new collective state.
(The per-ceremony privacy preamble has just completed; `TK_{H,F}`,
`TK_{H,IC}`, `TK_{F,IC}` are in place.)

### Phase 1 — Commitments (broadcast)

Each party builds its own secret polynomial — `f_H`, `f_F`,
`f_IC` — and broadcasts a *single* commitment value pinning that
polynomial down. Same bytes to every peer; that's what makes the
commitment public.

```
   H              F              IC
   │ ── commit_H ─►              │              H's commitment (commit_H)
   │ ── commit_H ───────────────►│              is a single value; the
   │              │              │              same bytes go to F and IC.
   │              │ ── commit_F ►│              F's commitment (commit_F):
   │ ◄── commit_F ─              │              same bytes to H and IC.
   │              │ ◄── commit_IC│              IC's commitment (commit_IC):
   │ ◄── commit_IC ──────────────              same bytes to H and F.
```
▷ Each party builds a **secret polynomial** `f_P` of degree
   `threshold − 1` whose constant term is its contribution to the
   joint secret. The single broadcast value `commit_P` is the
   polynomial's coefficients **lifted to points on the secp256k1
   curve** (a coefficient `a` becomes `a·G`). Recovering a scalar
   coefficient from its curve-point commitment would require
   solving the discrete-logarithm problem; meanwhile, anyone
   holding `commit_P` can later verify that evaluations of `f_P`
   behave correctly. After this phase every party holds `commit_H`,
   `commit_F`, and `commit_IC`, and no party knows any peer's
   coefficients.

   **Sensitivity profile.** Commitments are **public by design** —
   leaking `commit_P` to an outsider does not weaken the protocol.
   We still AEAD-wrap them under the pairwise `TK` on the wire,
   but only as defense-in-depth: uniform encryption everywhere is
   cheap and removes a traffic-shape side channel.
   🔒 *(defense-in-depth — not load-bearing)*

### Phase 2 — Subshares (private per pair)

Now each party evaluates its `f_P` at every peer's participant
index, producing a *tailored* scalar per recipient. Each one is
sent privately under the matching `TK`.

```
   H              F              IC
   │ ─ s_{H→F} ──►│              │
   │ ─ s_{H→IC} ─────────────────►
   │              │ ─ s_{F→H} ──►│       (notation: s_{P→Q} is
   │ ◄─ s_{F→H} ──│              │        P's polynomial f_P
   │              │ ─ s_{F→IC} ─►│        evaluated at Q's
   │ ◄─ s_{IC→H} ─────────────────       participant index, sent
   │              │ ◄─ s_{IC→F} ─│        privately to Q)
```
▷ Party `P` computes `s_{P→Q} = f_P(Q's index)` and sends it to
   peer `Q`. So `Q` receives one subshare from each contributor
   `P`. A single subshare under-determines `f_P` (the polynomial
   has `threshold` coefficients; one evaluation isn't enough to
   solve for them), so leaking one subshare reveals nothing
   about the sender's contribution; only collecting **all** of
   them — which only the sender could produce — would. After
   this phase every party holds one subshare from every peer
   plus a self-evaluation it computed locally.

   **No request/response dependency.** Each party can compute
   *all* its outgoing subshares from its own polynomial `f_P`
   alone — no input from peers is needed. So in our 3-party
   setup, all six subshares can be produced in parallel by their
   senders before any of them hits the wire. The arrows in the
   diagram running in both directions are concurrent independent
   messages, not request/response pairs.

   **Sensitivity profile.** Subshares are **genuinely private** —
   `s_{P→Q}` carries a piece of `P`'s polynomial that, combined
   with enough other evaluations, would reconstruct `f_P` and
   thus `P`'s contribution to the joint secret. Each subshare
   must reach the dedicated peer **and no one else** — not an
   outsider, not a different peer. The pairwise encryption is
   load-bearing here.
   🔒 *(load-bearing — must never escape the {P,Q} pair)*  🗝

### Phase 3 — Verify and aggregate (local)

```
   At party Q, for each received s_{P→Q}:
     check  s_{P→Q}·G  ==  commit_P[0] + Q·commit_P[1] + Q²·commit_P[2] + ...
     (right-hand side: commit_P "evaluated at Q's index" by
      treating its curve-point coefficients as a polynomial)

     If any check fails → abort, name P. The check is
     reproducible by anyone holding commit_P, so blame
     is unambiguous.

   If all checks pass:
     x_Q  ←  Σ_P  s_{P→Q}    (sum of received subshares)        🗝
```
▷ The verification asks one question per received subshare:
   **"is `s_{P→Q}` actually `f_P(Q's index)` for the same
   polynomial whose coefficients P committed to in Phase 1?"**
   Three pieces are in play:

   - `f_P` = `a₀ + a₁·x + a₂·x² + …` — the secret polynomial; P
     is the only party that knows the scalar coefficients.
   - `commit_P` = `[a₀·G, a₁·G, a₂·G, …]` — those coefficients
     **lifted to curve points**, broadcast publicly in Phase 1.
   - `s_{P→Q}` = `f_P(Q's index)` — a single scalar (an
     *evaluation*, not a coefficient), sent privately to Q in
     Phase 2.

   Q checks
   `s_{P→Q}·G  ==  commit_P[0] + Q·commit_P[1] + Q²·commit_P[2] + …`.
   Both sides compute the same value `f_P(Q's index)·G` by
   different routes — the LHS by lifting the claimed scalar to
   the curve; the RHS by evaluating the commitment's curve-point
   coefficients at Q's index. They agree iff the subshare and
   the commitment came from the same polynomial. If P substituted
   random bytes for the subshare, the LHS doesn't match. If P
   substituted random bytes for the commitment, every peer's
   check fails simultaneously (and the commitment was already
   public, so the lie cannot be retracted). Either way the check
   is reproducible by anyone holding `commit_P`, so blame is
   unambiguous.

   What this catches: ✅ a substituted subshare value;
   ✅ different polynomials' subshares sent to different peers
   (every peer's check uses the same `commit_P`).
   What it does not catch on its own: ❌ a sender genuinely
   picking a "bad" polynomial — that's outside the scope of
   VSS verification and addressed by other protocol mechanisms.

   This polynomial-vs-commitment trick (a "Verifiable Secret
   Sharing" scheme — Pedersen-style here) recurs across many
   threshold-cryptography protocols: BLS threshold signatures,
   FROST, polynomial commitment schemes like KZG, and others all
   exploit the same homomorphism between a polynomial in the
   scalar field and the same polynomial in curve points.

   Once everything verifies, each party **locally sums the
   subshares it received** to produce its share `x_Q`. The joint
   *public* key is the sum of every party's constant-term
   commitment (`commit_H[0] + commit_F[0] + commit_IC[0]`) —
   anyone who saw Phase 1 can compute it. The joint *private*
   key was never assembled; no party knows it.

### Phase 4 — Report up

```
   each party Q → coordinator :  ceremony/complete                  🔓
                                  { wallet-public-key,
                                    X_Q = x_Q·G }
```
▷ Coordinator confirms all three reported `wallet-public-key`
   values agree (they should, since all three parties sum the
   same `commit_*[0]`s). The wallet exists; each party stores
   its share `x_Q` locally, encrypted at rest under its
   identity-derived KEK.

### Why the phases are shaped this way

The "private piece" each party holds at the end (`x_Q`) must be
reconstructible only by the threshold and never by a single party
or by an outsider. The protocol gets that property by **spreading
each party's contribution across the others** (Phase 2 subshares
`s_{P→Q}`), with **public commitments that let receivers detect
tampering** (Phase 1 `commit_P`). Without Phase 1, a malicious
sender could substitute random bytes in Phase 2 and quietly render
the joint key unusable. Without Phase 2's privacy, an outsider
seeing all subshares could reconstruct `f_P` and the joint secret.

The actual implementation (`near/mpc`'s `threshold-signatures`
crate) compresses these phases with batching and proofs-of-knowledge,
so on the wire there are typically a handful of broadcast and
private messages per pair rather than discrete rounds — but the
conceptual roles above are what they're playing.

After this ceremony, the wallet is operational but cannot yet sign
anything: there is no presignature stockpile and the IC is now
offline. Two preparatory ceremonies (`triple-generation` and
`presign`) run between H and F to build a stockpile of one-shot
signing material before any signature is produced. Structurally
they look just like keygen — two parties, encrypted protocol
messages, an output stored locally — so they're not drawn here.

## Ceremony 2 — Routine Signing (2-of-3)

**Goal**: produce one ECDSA signature on a transaction message hash,
valid under the wallet's public key. The IC is **offline**; only H
and F participate. One presignature from the stockpile is consumed.

(The bootstrap preamble has just completed — the `begin-sign`
instruction carrying the digest, coordinator (= F), share-handle,
and presig-handle has been distributed to H and F, and one fresh
`TK_{H,F}` is in place. The IC was not contacted.)

### Phase 1 — OT-based ECDSA sign protocol

```
   H              F
   │ ─ proto ──── ►│        Both parties decrypt their share file
   │ ◄── proto ────│        and presignature file from disk into
   │ ─ proto ──── ►│        memory. They run a small number of
   │ ◄── proto ────│        protocol rounds that combine their
                              shares with the precomputed
                              presignature nonce, producing the
                              `r` and `s` components of the ECDSA
                              signature. The shares themselves
                              never leave either party.            🗝
```
▷ The protocol uses **oblivious transfer (OT)** to multiply the
   two parties' share scalars without either side learning the
   other's input. (OT is a primitive where a sender offers
   several possible values, the receiver picks one, and the
   sender learns nothing about which one was chosen — composed
   together via "OT extensions" and Beaver triples, this lets
   two parties compute a multiplication of their secrets and
   each end up holding additive shares of the product.) The
   `r` half of the ECDSA signature is read directly from the
   presignature; the `s` half is the multiplied-share output
   adjusted by the message digest. Only intermediate masking
   randomness flows on the wire; the share scalars `x_H`, `x_F`
   and the presignature's nonce never leave their respective
   parties. Result: one ECDSA signature `(r, s)` held by the
   coordinator (F), and a consumed presignature on both parties.

### Phase 2 — Report up + cleanup

```
   F → coordinator : ceremony/complete  { signature = r ‖ s }       🔓
   H → coordinator : ceremony/complete  (no payload)                🔓

   on both parties, locally:
     - presignature file deleted from disk (one-time-use).
```
▷ Coordinator independently verifies the signature against the
   wallet's public key (using a stock ECDSA verifier — no MPC
   trust). Wallet's persistent state advances by one consumed
   presignature.

### Why this can sign without the IC

The wallet was created as a **2-of-3** scheme: any two of {H, F, IC}
holding their shares can collectively produce a signature; one share
alone cannot. So routine signing only needs H + F online, which is
the operational point — the IC stays cold-storage between structural
ceremonies (recovery, divorce, refresh) and is therefore harder to
compromise.

### Why each presignature is one-time-use

A presignature embeds a precomputed nonce. Each ECDSA signature
relies on its nonce being unique and unpredictable; using one
presignature for two different signatures with two different message
digests would let an outside observer derive the share scalars from
public r-values. The harness enforces one-shot use by deleting the
presignature file on consumption.

## What the coordinator role never sees

Whether the coordinator role is held by the orchestrator (this
harness) or by a rotating party (production), it sees only:

- Cleartext envelope metadata for routing.
- Opaque ciphertext for everything between peers.
- Public outputs declared in `ceremony/complete`: public keys,
  signatures, verification shares.

It does not see private shares, ephemeral DH keys, intermediate
protocol state, long-term identity private keys, or the plaintext
form of any persisted artifact. This is what makes the role
rotatable and makes the ceremony composition robust to a single-
party compromise.

  `─⇋─`. See `signet/docs/05-noise-kk-session-design.md` for the
  message-by-message walkthrough.

  signing stockpile. Structurally identical to keygen at this
  level of abstraction.

  structural ceremonies in the system; deserve their own walkthrough.

  signatures crate. By design, none of that surfaces here.
